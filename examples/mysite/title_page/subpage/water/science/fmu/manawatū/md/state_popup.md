# Diving deeper: how we calculate state

To get a reliable picture of our waterways, scientists need much more than a single water sample. Water quality constantly shifts due to rainfall, seasons, and land use changes. 

### The five-year hydrological snapshot
We calculate the current state of a river using data collected over the five hydrological years ending 1st July 2025. A hydrological year runs from July to June, aligning with natural water cycles rather than the standard calendar year. 

Before any analysis begins, the raw data undergoes strict quality control. Using National Environmental Monitoring Standards (NEMS), scientists screen the dataset to remove any missing or compromised measurements to ensure the final calculations are highly accurate.

### Minimum data requirements
To receive an official grade, a monitoring site must meet specific data requirements:
*   **Chemical and bacterial tests:** We require a site to have at least 30 out of 60 expected monthly samples over the five-year period to calculate its state. This is slightly less strict than some national reporting platforms, but it allows us to include and track more of our local waterways.
*   **Ecological health:** Biological indicators, like the Macroinvertebrate Community Index (MCI), are typically only measured once a year. For these metrics, a site needs at least four valid data points over the five-year window to calculate a reliable state.

### Making sense of the metrics
When analysing this five-year dataset, scientists use specific statistical metrics to understand how a river behaves under different conditions:
*   **The median (the typical day):** The median is the middle value of all collected samples, representing what the water quality is like on a normal, average day. Our 30-sample minimum works very well for calculating a highly reliable median.
*   **The 95th percentile (the extremes):** This metric focuses on the higher end of the data, representing a value where 95% of the samples fall below it. Practically, it reveals what the water is like on a "bad" day, such as during heavy rainfall when surface runoff washes elevated levels of pollution into the river. Because we allow a smaller minimum dataset (30 samples), calculating these rare, extreme spikes can sometimes carry slightly more statistical uncertainty than calculating the median.

### Attribute bands and the "worst-case" rule
The final calculated metrics are evaluated against the National Policy Statement for Freshwater Management 2020 (NPS-FM 2020). This national framework assigns an absolute score, or attribute band, ranging from A (good) to E (poor). 

For certain indicators, the NPS-FM requires scientists to evaluate multiple statistics, such as both the median and the 95th percentile. When this happens, the final grade is always determined by the worst-performing statistic. For example, if a river's median pollution level scores an 'A', but its 95th percentile spikes score a 'C', the site receives an overall 'C' grade. This strict approach ensures we do not overlook occasional but harmful spikes in pollution, providing a highly protective view of our ecosystem health.
